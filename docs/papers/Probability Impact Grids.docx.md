# From PIGs to Monte-Carlo in 3-Pages

# **Summary**

This short paper highlights some recent thoughts, ideas and trends in risk management.  These include the inadequacy of probability-impact grids, the move towards uncertainty management, the value of valuing information and new approaches around an old tool: Monte Carlo simulation.

The paper encourages Northumbrian Water Group risk management team to review current enterprise risk management process in light of these trends.

## **Criticism**

Probability impact grids (PIGs) are used by many organizations as a core tool of their enterprise risk management process and this is the case within Northumbrian Water Group.

There exists within the risk management sector a group of practitioners and researchers who have shone a critical light on probability impact grids.  Chris Chapman and Stephen Ward, both Professors of Management at  the University of Southampton, are part of this group and describe the concerns in a section of the 3rd\-edition of their book *How to Manage Project Opportunity and Risk* as follows:

“The probability-impact grid (PIG) – a tool that needs scrapping.

PIGs suffer from major inherent limitations (Cox, 2008; Hubbard, 2009), and require a simplistic characterisation of risk and uncertainty that falls well short of a minimum clarity requirement.  As Cox (2008) concludes, ‘the meaning of a risk matrix may be far from transparent, despite its simple appearance’, but worse, their evidently attractive simplicity can discourage efforts to produce a more meaningful and useful analysis of uncertainty and risk.  Their continued use involves a classic ‘tail wagging the dog’ illustration of much poor ‘risk management’ practice, and their use should now be scrapped in all contexts, along with all definitions of risk, opportunity or uncertainty designed to accommodate them.”

Not often will such an unequivocal statement be found in a management book.  Were this written by practitioners out to make a headline and get noticed then we might not pay much attention.  However, Chapman and Ward have extensive careers in risk management and have worked with some of the UKs leading companies and organizations such as BP, the Highways Agency and the ICE.  They have credibility and this means we would be foolish not to listen to what they have to say.

Hubbard, referenced by Chapman and Ward, was equally unequivocal in his book *The Failure of Risk Management* and uses an entire chapter to highlight many of the major flaws with PIGs:

“Worse than Useless: the Most Popular Risk Assessment Method and Why It Doesn’t Work.

… Unfortunately, any perceived benefit of this approach may be mostly a placebo effect. Even worse, the method itself may violate the ‘‘First, do no harm’’ principle of decision analysis by adding its own sources of error and making the decision worse than it would have been. Dr. Tony Cox, who holds a PhD in risk analysis from MIT, has researched these methods probably more than anyone else and has concluded that they are often ‘‘worse than useless.’’

## **Uncertainty**

The secondary title of Chapman and Ward’s book is “*Why Uncertainty Management Can be a Much Better Approach than Risk Mangagment”* reflecting another trend towards uncertainty management using probabilistic methods rather than risk event management using PIGs.  

In the Foreword to Chapman and Ward Mike Nichols, Chair APM and Chair BSI Standards Policy Committee, states:

“risk management has unduly and prematurely concentrated on identifying and analyzing risk events.  A much better return from the effort involved is first to assess systematically the information needed for decisions in pursuit of objectives”

Chapman and Ward state:

Effective management of corporate performance in any context requires an appreciation of the sources of uncertainty that can have a significant effect on future performance at a level of decomposition which reflects their importance and corporate capability to do something about them … uncertainty includes events, ambiguity, inherent variability and systemic uncertainty.  …  Risk events are often the least important uncertainties.  … Organisations that successfully adopt an uncertainty management perspective will have a significant advantage over those that do not”.

## **Value of Information**

The value of information is covered in another book by Hubbard titled *How to Measure Anything* and by Stanford Professor of Management Sam Savage in his book *The Flaw of Averages*.

Savage writes:

“Information has no value unless it has the potential to change a decision.  Information is the complement of uncertainty, that is, for every uncertainty, there is information that would reduce or remove it.”

Given that uncertainty is reduced by information and decisions should be made on the basis of information it follows that pertinent information should be gathered before making a decision.  However, information gathering costs money therefore the question arises about how much money should be spent gathering information to reduce uncertainty prior to making a decision?  

Decision theory allows the value of information to be calculated and often it is found that organisations are overspending in gathering irrelevant information and does not reduce decision making uncertainty.

## **Probabilistic Uncertainty Management**

All the authors thus far mentioned in this paper are strong advocates of managing uncertainty probabilistically using some well known but not so often used tools.  These include making range estimates rather than point value estimates, using calibrated estimators or calibrated subject matter experts, and using Monte Carlo simulations to aggregate uncertainties and provide confidences around outcomes.

Savage and Hubbard are involved with an exciting new not-for-profit called [http://www.probabilitymanagement.org](http://www.probabilitymanagement.org)  that is promoting some simple but powerful tools and processes for achieving organisation wide competence in probability uncertainty management.  Corporate sponsors are US-based and include Lockheed Martin, GE and Chevron.  The Judge Business School Centre for Risk Studies at Cambridge University is an affiliate.

## **Conclusion**

This short report is simply a sign-post to the processes and approaches that a group of maverick but inspirational risk practitioners are now advocating.  

The main message is that the PIG is dead.

The secondary message is that certain organizations are recognising this and are beginning to build the competence needed to master the ‘newer’ approaches based around Monte Carlo simulation.

Northumbrian Water should be open to critically reviewing its current approach to enterprise risk management and is encouraged to investigate and evaluate the approaches researchers at notable US and UK institutions are now advocating.

## **Personal**

I’ve been involved in safety and risk management for over 20-years and have not used a probability impact grid in over 18 years.  I was never convinced they worked.  In personal safety we aren’t really dealing with much uncertainty because we know what hurts people.  The approach is more towards compliance management and managing portfolios of risk controls.  More recently I was building out competency in using simple Monte Carlo simulations with project managers within Siemens Service Renewables because the project plans were almost always behind schedule and over budget.
