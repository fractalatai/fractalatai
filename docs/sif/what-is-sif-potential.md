# What is SIF potential?

SIF stands for Serious Injury or Fatality. SIF *potential* is the question: **could this event have killed or permanently injured someone — regardless of what actually happened?**

## Why "potential" matters more than outcome

Traditional safety metrics count what happened: someone broke an arm, someone needed stitches, someone went to hospital. SIF potential asks a different question — not what *did* happen, but what *could* have happened given the energy that was present.

A scaffold board falls 20 metres and lands on empty ground. Nobody is hurt. On a traditional incident report this is a near-miss with zero severity. But 20 metres of gravitational energy hitting a person's head is fatal. The energy was there. The only reason nobody died is that nobody was standing underneath at that moment.

That near-miss has the same SIF potential as a fatality from the same event. The energy was identical. Only the path differed.

## The two probabilities

Every incident involves two distinct probabilities:

**Chance P** — the probability the energy reaches a person at all. Did the falling object hit someone? Did the worker actually fall? This is about whether the event *happened to someone*.

**Outcome P** — given the energy *did* reach a person, what range of injuries could result? This is about how bad it would be.

SIF potential analysis sets chance P = 1. It says: "assume the energy reached a person. Now, what is the distribution of possible outcomes?" This is deliberate. In a near-miss, the energy was present and available. SIF analysis looks past the lucky draw (a miss) and examines what was at stake.

## Why this changes how we think about near-misses

On a probability-impact grid, a near-miss with no injury scores low on the impact axis — there was no impact. This buries events that were one second or one metre from being fatalities.

With SIF potential, a near-miss where a scaffold board fell 20 metres onto empty ground scores the same as a fatality from a scaffold board falling 20 metres onto someone's head. Both have P(SIF) ≈ 0.85. The system treats them equally because the energy hazard was equal. This is how organisations find and fix the conditions that kill people before they actually kill someone.

## What SIF potential ignores — and why

SIF potential deliberately ignores safety controls (guardrails, safety nets, PPE, lockout/tagout). This sounds counterintuitive — shouldn't we credit good controls?

The reasoning: SIF potential asks "what if the barriers failed or weren't present?" In a near-miss, they *nearly did* fail. Controls are not 100% reliable. A guardrail that was present today might not be tomorrow. By assessing the uncontrolled energy, the system identifies where the stakes are highest, regardless of current controls. The controls are then assessed separately — how effective are they, and what is the residual risk with them in place? That is the job of the [simulator](monte-carlo-and-swiss-cheese.md).
